What you are hearing and seeing

One pure tone moves smoothly from 432 Hz to 440 Hz and back. Each nine-second cycle repeats three times. Both viewings use identical audio and the same visual response; the second adds pitch labels.

The sound keeps the same amplitude, with brief fades at cycle boundaries to avoid clicks. Your device and hearing can still affect perceived loudness. This compares a single tone, rather than a whole piece of music tuned to either reference.

The visual is a mathematical model of a vibrating membrane, not filmed sand or water. The surface and its driving point stay fixed while the pitch changes. Color highlights relatively low motion, and contrast is adjusted to keep each pattern visible. It does not measure absolute vibration strength.

This surface was chosen to show a changing response across this small pitch range. Different dimensions, tension, materials, or driving points would produce different patterns. Neither 432 nor 440 Hz has one universal cymatic shape, and neither is automatically more coherent.

Repetition, familiarity, and the starting pitch may also influence what you notice. This is an invitation to explore perception, not a test of which tuning is better.

THE HISTORY BEHIND THE COMPARISON

One note.
More than one reference.

A tuning reference gives musicians
a shared starting point.

Here, A means the A above middle C. At 432 Hz, it vibrates 432 times per second. At 440 Hz, it vibrates 440 times per second. Other notes are tuned in relation to that reference.

A=432 has a real place in tuning history.
It was not the previous global standard.

1859 · France chooses A=435

France established A=435 Hz as its official reference, known as the diapason normal. This became an influential alternative to the many pitches then used by different musical institutions. [2]

1881–1884 · A=432 in Italy

An Italian musicians’ congress adopted A=432 in 1881. Giuseppe Verdi supported the lower reference amid concerns about consistency and strain on singers. In 1884, the Italian War Ministry prescribed A=432 for orchestral and military ensembles. These were significant Italian endorsements, not a worldwide agreement. [1]

1939 · An international agreement on A=440

A conference in London agreed on A=440 Hz as the international reference. It was already used in the United States before that meeting. The agreement was a milestone in standardization, not an abrupt worldwide replacement of A=432. Different pitch practices continued. [2] [4]

1955 onward · The standard is carried forward

ISO adopted A=440 as a recommendation in 1955. ISO 16:1975 specifies the same reference pitch. A shared standard helps musicians coordinate; it does not determine which listening experience you must prefer. [2] [3]
